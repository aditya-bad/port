## My call

 The short answer
 
Build Linck as the platform where Indian F&O traders write a strategy once and run it on the same engine through backtest, paper, shadow and live, and where every rupee it reports has been checked against what the market would really have given.
 
That last part is the company. Lots of platforms run strategies. None of them can show that their paper P&L matches what live trading would have done, and none of them hand you a list of every place your strategy’s day went wrong. Linck already has most of that today: the day check, repair, replay, decision logs, the fault matrix and strategies that report on themselves. The platform turns it into the product.
 
Contracts final from day one; implementations grow into them. We build the intermediate architecture now, behind the final architecture’s interfaces, and swap each piece when its trigger arrives. No big-bang rewrite, ever.

### In what order

- First, a managed runtime for serious retail option traders: “we run your strategies”. They use our strategies or write their own with the SDK, paper first, then live through their own broker account.
 
- Then the open SDK and public API for developers, quants and small desks, with a backtest farm and shadow mode.
 
- Then brokers (B2B): they white-label Linck for their own customers.
 
- Marketplace last, once the regulatory path for selling strategies to others is in place.

### What makes it hard, and why it’s worth it

- The market’s rules, data licensing and broker limits shape the system more than any technology choice does (section 04).
 
- Running other people’s code next to money needs sandboxing, budgets and one writer of money from the start.
 
- One engine for every mode is a lot of up-front work. It’s also the only way “paper = live” can ever be true, and that is the company’s moat.

## Rules of the market

These decide the architecture before any technology does. This isn’t legal advice: each one needs a lawyer and the current circulars before launch.

Algos go through a brokerSEBI’s 2025 framework for retail algos, phased in by the exchanges, makes the broker responsible. API orders come from registered static IPs, every algo order is tagged with an exchange-approved algo ID, and access goes through broker-issued keys, not open APIs. Linck is built so every order carries its algo ID and leaves through a known IP.
 
White box or black boxA strategy whose logic the user can see is treated differently from one whose logic is hidden. Selling hidden-logic strategies to others brings registration (as a research analyst) and record-keeping duties. That is why the marketplace comes last and why “open box” is a product value.
 
Order-rate thresholdAbove a threshold set by the exchanges (around 10 orders a second per client), a self-written algo has to be registered. The risk engine enforces each account’s limit, so no strategy can cross it by accident.
 
Market data is licensedA user’s Kite feed is for that user. A shared feed for thousands of users needs an exchange-authorised data vendor and a redistribution licence. Market data is built as a set of plug-in feeds so this is a contract, not a rewrite. It is likely our largest bill: get quotes early.
 
Broker limitsBrokers cap sockets, instruments per socket, orders per second and orders per day. Kite, for example, allows 3 WebSocket connections of 3,000 instruments each per API key. These limits live only in the broker adapters and the risk engine.
 
Records are kept for yearsOrder, algo and audit trails are kept for the regulatory period on write-once storage. Deleting a user’s account never deletes those records; their personal data is separated so it can be erased.
 
Personal dataIndia’s DPDP Act covers consent, purpose and erasure. Data stays in India, at sites the user picks (any provider or own servers), with disaster recovery at a second one. Brokers buying the B2B product will also ask for SOC 2 or ISO 27001 and the cyber-resilience controls they themselves must meet.
 
Paper is unregulated, live isn’tPaper trading for anyone is simple. Live trading with each user’s own broker account, for their own strategy, is the cleanest first live path. Running our strategies with other people’s money comes later, with the broker partnership.

## Principles

The few rules that make “plug and play” real rather than a slogan.

Contracts firstEvery boundary is a versioned protobuf contract in one schema repository: strategy API, market data, broker adapter, risk rule, orders and fills. New code is written against the contract; a contract changes only by adding.
 
Everything is a pluginBrokers, data feeds, strategies, indicators, risk rules, execution algorithms, fill models, notifiers, Geek views and reports all register through an extension point, and each has a test kit it must pass (section 09).
 
One engine for every modeBacktest, replay, repair, paper, shadow and live run the same strategy code through the same runtime. Only the clock, the market and the broker objects change. Linck’s replay and repair already work this way.
 
One writer of moneyOnly the order service writes orders, fills, positions and cash. Repairs, manual closes and the marketplace all go through it.
 
Compute once, share everywhereEach instrument is subscribed once, each bar built once and each indicator, implied volatility and option-chain view computed once per cell. Thousands of strategies read the result.
 
Cells for blast radiusTenants are pinned to cells: self-contained copies of the runtime, market cache, order partition and ledger shard. A bad cell hurts its tenants, never everyone. Growth means adding cells.
 
Nothing silentEvery failure is either handled or reported, with a day attached so it lands on the Repair page. This is the fault matrix and the self-reporting from Linck today, made a platform rule.
 
DeterminismGiven the same ticks, a strategy version makes the same decisions, so any day can be replayed exactly. Clocks and randomness come from the runtime, never the system.

### Market data fabric
Rust
 
Feed handlers per vendor or broker decode and normalise into one compact record. A sequencer numbers each instrument’s updates. A tick cache in shared memory on every runtime host holds the latest state per instrument and a ring of recent updates.
 OwnsSubscriptions (counted across everyone), the tick logIf it failsA second vendor takes over; strategies hold on stale prices and say so

### Computed streams
Rust
 
Bars, indicators, implied volatility and greeks, and option-chain views (ATM by the future, strike by premium), each computed once per cell and read by every strategy that asks. A strategy that wants SuperTrend(7,3) on NIFTY 5-minute bars subscribes to it; it doesn’t compute it.
 OwnsThe catalogue of computed seriesWhyMost strategies watch the same few hundred contracts: compute once, share

### Strategy runtime
Rust supervisor
 
Runs every deployment: a supervisor per host hands each strategy its events (ticks, bars, timers, fills) in order, saves its state after each event, and enforces CPU, memory and order budgets. Strategies run in sandboxes: Python in gVisor, WASM components in Wasmtime, or remotely over gRPC.
 OwnsLive strategy state, leasesIf it failsLeases move to another host, which resumes from the last snapshot

### Orders & risk
Go
 
The only writer of money. Pre-trade risk (loss limits, size, margin, order rate, market hours, kill switches), multi-leg orders placed hedge-first, splitting above freeze limits, routing to a broker or the sim venue, and fills written with an outbox row in one transaction.
 OwnsOrders, fills, positions, lots, cash (the ledger shard)If it failsIntents wait on the backbone; nothing lost or doubled

Rust
 
Paper trading as just another broker. It fills from order-book depth with a queue and latency model, applies the exchange’s rules (price bands, tick size, freeze limits), and produces shadow fills next to every live order. The same library runs inside backtests.
 OwnsThe fill model and its versionsWhy its ownPaper, backtest, replay and shadow must fill identically

### Broker adapters
Go
 
One contract, one adapter per broker: place, modify, cancel, positions, margins, order updates, and the broker’s own limits. A new broker is a new adapter plus its test kit, nothing else.
 OwnsEach broker’s quirks and limitsIf it failsThat broker’s users get an issue; nothing else is touched

Go · Python
 
The trust layer, and the product’s heart: shadow comparisons, the end-of-day check, repair cases, reconciliation with each broker, and every issue strategies report. It is today’s Repair page, grown into a service.

## Running every strategy, efficiently

The heart of the platform: many feeds in, tens of thousands of strategies out, without a queue building anywhere.

[Diagram: The tick path. Feeds go to Rust feed handlers that normalise each update, then to a sequencer that writes a durable log (later the lake) and sends updates to every cell. Each runtime host keeps a tick cache in shared memory, computed streams build bars, indicators, implied volatility and chain views once, strategy sandboxes receive only the newest values at their chosen cadence, and their intents go to orders and risk.]

| Sizing assumption (year 3) | Number | What it forces | 

| Users on a trading day | 30k of 100k | A stateless edge and control plane; tenants spread over cells | 
 
| Strategies running | 50k | 8–15 cells of a few thousand strategies; placement by the scheduler | 
 
| Instruments watched | ~60k | Every instrument subscribed once; options cluster near the money, so overlap is huge | 
 
| Updates in at the open | 100–300k / s | Rust handlers, a binary record, no per-message allocation, no JSON | 
 
| Strategy events out | 50–200k / s | After conflation. Python at ~1 ms an event is 50–200 cores, spread over cells | 
 
| Exits at 15:15 | ~150k orders | Orders partitioned by account; per-broker, per-key rate buckets; shorts closed before longs | 
 
| Fills | 2–5M / day | Ledger sharded by cell, partitioned by month | 
 
| 1-minute bars | ~22M rows / day | ClickHouse and the lake, never the ledger | 

### What makes it efficient

- Compute once. 5,000 strategies wanting “NIFTY ATM straddle premium” or “EMA(20) on 1-hour bars” cost one computation, not 5,000.
 
- Option chains are local. Today an entry asks Kite for the chain and can fail when Kite does (the fault we fixed with retries). Here the chain view is in memory, so lookups take microseconds and can’t fail at the entry minute.
 
- Placement by overlap. The scheduler puts strategies that watch the same instruments on the same host, so a host needs only a slice of the market.
 
- Cadence is declared. A strategy chooses every tick, every 250 ms, on bar close, or on a timer. Most need bars; few need ticks.
 
- Hot and cold. A strategy waiting for 15:15 sleeps on a timer and costs nothing until then.

### The two minutes that matter

- 09:15. At 09:00 the scheduler reads every deployment’s plan, pre-subscribes their instruments, warms chains and wakes the strategies, so the open is a burst of decisions rather than of set-up.
 
- 15:15. Every intraday exit lands in the same minute. Orders queue by account, drain within each broker’s limits, close risk first (shorts before longs, hedges last), and report anything not done by 15:20.
 
- Rehearsed daily. The previous day’s open and close are replayed against the new release before it ships.

## The SDK

What a user writes, and the path every strategy takes from a laptop to live money.

from linck import Strategy, param, on, State
from datetime import time

class ShortStraddle(Strategy):
 """Sell the ATM straddle at the entry time; per-leg stop; square off at 15:15."""
 underlying = param("NIFTY")
 entry = param(time(9, 20))
 stop = param(0.30, min=0.05, max=1.0, label="Stop, % of premium")

 state = State(entered_on=None) # saved by the platform after every event

 def subscribe(self, ctx):
 return [ctx.index(self.underlying).bars("1m")]

 @on.bar
 async def bar(self, ctx, bar):
 if ctx.now.time() < self.entry or ctx.state.entered_on == ctx.today:
 return ctx.decide("entry", "waiting")
 chain = ctx.chain(self.underlying, expiry="this_week") # local: no broker call
 atm = chain.atm(by="future")
 await ctx.orders.place([atm.ce.sell(lots=1), atm.pe.sell(lots=1)],
 tag="entry", stop=self.stop)
 ctx.state.entered_on = ctx.today

 @on.time("15:15")
 async def square_off(self, ctx):
 await ctx.orders.close_all(reason="square_off")
 
The param declarations become the deploy form in the app, with types, limits and labels. The ctx object is the only door to the world: clock, market, chain, orders, state, decisions and reports. Swap ctx and the same code runs in a backtest, a replay or live.

### From a laptop to live money

- Write with the SDK; linck init scaffolds a strategy with tests.laptop
 
- Run a real day locally: linck run --day 2026-10-14 replays that day’s ticks through the same engine.seconds
 
- Backtest and sweep on the farm: linck backtest --from 2024-01 --sweep stop=0.2:0.4:0.05, with point-in-time chains, and walk-forward to catch overfitting.minutes
 
- Certify: the fault matrix (bad ticks, feed gaps, broker down, failed fills, lost state), determinism, CPU and memory budgets. It must pass to deploy.minutes
 
- Paper with fills from the sim venue.days
 
- Shadow: paper orders priced against the live book next to what Kite would really have done, with a running gap per trade.weeks
 
- Live, through the user’s broker, starting at 1 lot with a loss limit, and raised on the user’s say.ongoing
 
- Watched: the day check, issues, drift from its backtest, and repair when needed.every day

### Languages a user can write in

- Python, native and first: the quant ecosystem lives there. It runs in a gVisor sandbox with the SDK talking to the supervisor over a local socket.
 
- Rust, Go, TypeScript, C++ compiled to WASM components, run in Wasmtime: strong isolation, millisecond start-up and deterministic by construction.
 
- Anything, remotely: a strategy running on the user’s own machine connects over a gRPC stream and receives the same events. That’s handy for a quant’s own models; the platform still owns risk and orders.

### What the platform owns, so the user doesn’t

- State: declared fields saved after every event, so no hand-written save and restore.
 
- Versions: every deploy is a signed, immutable version; old days replay on the version that traded them.
 
- Decisions and reports: ctx.decide and ctx.report feed Geek mode, issues and alerts.
 
- Secrets: a strategy never sees a broker token.

## Plug and play

Every way to extend Linck, each with one contract and a test kit it must pass. Adding a feature means adding a plugin, not editing the core.

| Extension point | Contract | Written in | Must pass | 

| Strategy | SDK events in, intents, decisions and reports out | Python · WASM · remote | Fault matrix, determinism, budgets | 
 
| Broker adapter | Place, modify, cancel, positions, margins, order updates, limits | Go | Broker kit: rejects, partial fills, duplicates, outages, rate limits | 
 
| Data feed | Subscribe, records out, gaps and health | Rust | Feed kit: gaps, bad prices, reorder, reconnect | 
 
| Computed stream | Inputs, parameters, outputs and warm-up | Rust · Python | Same values as the reference over a recorded month | 
 
| Risk rule | Order and account in, allow, reduce or block | Go · CEL expressions | Never slower than a set budget; never fails open | 
 
| Execution algorithm | Parent order in, child orders out (legging, slicing, iceberg) | Go | Sim venue kit | 
 
| Fill model | Order and book in, fills out | Rust | Calibration against real fills; versioned | 
 
| Integrity check | A day in, findings out | Python · SQL | Known-bad days are caught, known-good pass | 
 
| Geek view & report | A declared query plus a chart spec | SQL · TypeScript | Renders on phone and desktop, both themes | 
 
| Notifier | An issue in, a delivery out | Go | Dedup and retry kit | 

### Adding a new service

- Start from a template in Go, Rust or Python: health checks, metrics, tracing, auth and the proto client are already wired in.
 
- Define its contract in the schema repository; generated clients appear for every language.
 
- Register it in the service catalogue with an owner, SLOs and the topics it may read and write.
 
- Ship behind a feature flag per tenant, then per cell.

### Adding a new feature

- Most features are a read model plus a view: new data from the backbone into ClickHouse, a declared query, a chart.
 
- Features that act (a new exit type, a new alert) are plugins at one of the points above.
 
- Nothing a feature does can write money except through the order service.

## How it talks

The protocol follows the job: shared memory where nanoseconds matter, gRPC where a caller waits, logs where nothing may be lost.

| Path | Mechanism | Guarantee | Why | 

| Feeds → handlers → cells | Binary records over UDP multicast or Aeron inside the site | Sequenced; gaps refilled from the log | Hundreds of thousands a second with no broker in the way | 
 
| Tick cache → strategies | Shared memory (seqlock + ring) on the host | Latest value; ring for “everything since” | No copies, no syscalls per tick | 
 
| Strategy ⇄ supervisor | Local socket, flatbuffers or protobuf | In order per strategy | Sandboxes have no network | 
 
| Runtime → orders | Redpanda orders.intent, keyed by account | At least once + idempotency key + lease epoch | An order can never be lost, doubled or sent by two runners | 
 
| Orders → everyone | Redpanda orders.fill via the outbox | At least once, ordered per account | Runtime, integrity, analytics and notifications all learn of each fill | 
 
| Service ⇄ service | gRPC with deadlines and mTLS | Request / response | Typed, fast, generated clients | 
 
| Control → cells | NATS subjects | At least once | Deploy, pause, kill switch, config changes | 
 
| Clients → platform | REST/JSON (OpenAPI) and gRPC | Request / response | REST for people and scripts, gRPC for the SDK | 
 
| Platform → clients | WebSocket; gRPC stream for the SDK; webhooks | Latest value, batched every 250 ms | Prices and P&L without polling | 
 
| Long jobs | Temporal workflows | Each step done once | End-of-day chain, backtests, repairs, payouts | 

Why both Redpanda and NATS: Redpanda (Kafka-compatible, no JVM) is the durable, replayable log for money and the tick archive, kept for days and re-readable. NATS is the cheap, fast fan-out for control messages and live updates to screens. Each does the job it’s best at.

## Data

Each store does one job. Only the ledger holds money.

| Store | Holds | Why this one | 

| Postgres (control) | Tenants, users, versions, deployments, policies, licences | Relational, small, transactional | 
 
| Postgres (ledger shards) | Orders, fills, positions, lots, cash: one shard per cell | Money needs transactions and constraints; sharding by cell keeps each one small. TigerBeetle was considered for double-entry speed and isn’t needed at these rates. |

### Tenancy

Every row carries its tenant, Postgres row-level security enforces it, and a tenant lives in one cell. Brokers (B2B) get their own cells, and their own database if they ask.

### Point in time

A backtest on 14 Mar 2024 sees the option chain, lot size, expiry calendar and freeze limit of that day, and nothing that came later.

## Every case we have to handle

What goes wrong in this business, and what the platform does about each one. Each row becomes a test, the way the fault matrix covers strategies today.

| Case | What the platform does | 

| The market | 
 
| Expiry day, weekly and monthly | The market calendar service says which contracts expire; strategies ask it, never work it out themselves. | 
 
| Holiday, special session (Muhurat), shortened day | One calendar, kept by us and checked against exchange circulars; strategies get the session, not the clock. | 
 
| Circuit, halt, price band hit | The sim venue rejects as the exchange would; live rejects become issues; strategies get an event, not an exception. | 
 
| Lot size, freeze limit or strike interval changes | Point-in-time reference data; orders above a freeze limit are split by the order service. | 
 
| Illiquid strike, no seller at the hedge | The chain view marks liquidity; shadow mode shows “not filled”; a hedge-first order doesn’t sell the short without its hedge. | 
 
| Gap open, VIX spike | Risk rules on margin and exposure; the integrity service flags strategies outside their backtested range. | 
 
| Data and brokers | 
 
| Vendor feed down | A second vendor takes over the subscriptions; strategies hold on stale prices and say so; the gap goes on the day’s record. | 
 
| Bad ticks, duplicates, out of order | Feed handlers drop them and hold spikes until confirmed, as Linck does today. | 
 
| Broker API down or slow | That broker’s adapter trips; its users’ strategies pause entries and keep exits queued; an issue per user. | 
 
| Broker token expired at 09:14 | Tokens are checked at 08:45 with a push asking the user to log in; strategies wait instead of failing. | 
 
| Partial fill, reject, duplicate fill report | The order service’s state machine handles each; idempotency keys stop doubles; strategies get the real state. | 
 
| Our book and the broker’s disagree | Reconciliation every few minutes; the broker wins; an issue says exactly what differs. | 
 
| Strategies | 
 
| Infinite loop, memory leak | CPU and memory budgets per event and per minute; the sandbox is rested, restarted from its snapshot, and reported. | 
 
| A strategy firing 1,000 orders a second | The account’s order-rate limit in the risk engine; kill switch at the threshold. | 
 
| A crash mid-trade | Restarted from its last snapshot and checked against the book, then re-synced (Linck’s leg reconciliation, kept). | 
 
| A creator pushes a buggy update | Subscribers stay on their pinned version; updates go out to a few subscribers first, then to the rest. | 
 
| Lookahead or overfitting in a backtest | Point-in-time data, walk-forward by default, and the shadow record shown next to the backtest. | 
 
| The platform | 
 
| A bad release at 10:00 | No releases 09:00–15:35 except hotfixes; each release replays yesterday first; a canary cell; rollback in one step. | 
 
| A cell or zone lost | Leases move hosts within seconds; a cell spans three zones; the ledger fails over with no lost commits. | 
 
| Site lost | The standby site: exits through the brokers’ own tools first, then the platform restored. | 
 
| Noisy neighbour | Budgets per strategy, quotas per tenant, cells per broker partner. | 
 
| Clock drift | Hosts sync to a disciplined time source; exchange timestamps are the truth; drift alarms. | 
 
| Money and people | 
 
| Payment fails with live positions | No new entries; exits always allowed; never close someone’s position over a bill. | 
 
| Account deleted with open positions | Deletion waits until flat; personal data is erased, trading records kept as the law requires. | 
 
| A user’s daily loss limit hit | New entries blocked, existing strategies told; the user chooses to flatten or not, unless their own rule says flatten. | 
 
| Regulator or broker asks for a trail | Every order with its algo ID, strategy version, decision and the ticks it saw, exported from the audit store. | 
 
| Fraud or pump schemes in the marketplace | Listings need a shadow-verified record, a disclosed logic summary and certification; payouts are held for a period. |

## Reliability & security

### Running it

- Where: the user chooses: any cloud, their own servers, or a mix. Kubernetes per cell (any conformant one), disaster recovery at a second site of their choosing, Helm charts and GitOps (Argo CD or Flux) for releases. No provider is assumed.
 
- SLOs: tick to intent p99 under 50 ms in a cell; no strategy silent for over 60 s in market hours; 15:15 exits drained by 15:20; end-of-day checks by 16:30; zero unreconciled positions.
 
- Observability: OpenTelemetry from tick to decision to order to fill, in one trace; Prometheus, Grafana, Loki; one dashboard per cell.
 
- Practice: every release replays yesterday’s open and close; game days in the evening break a feed, a broker and a cell on purpose.

### Keeping it safe

- Untrusted code: gVisor or Wasmtime sandboxes, no network, read-only file system, seccomp, budgets; it can only talk to the supervisor.
 
- Secrets: broker tokens encrypted per tenant in Vault; only the adapters can decrypt them; strategies never see one.
 
- Supply chain: signed builds, SBOMs and dependency pinning, for our code and for uploaded strategies.
 
- People: SSO with hardware keys for staff, just-in-time production access, every admin action audited.
 
- Proof: SOC 2 Type II and ISO 27001 before selling to brokers; yearly penetration tests; a bug bounty.

## Risks

What kills companies like this, and what we do about each one.

A trust-breaking incidentOne bug that loses users real money ends the brand. Defence: one writer of money, certification, shadow before live, loss limits on by default, small first live sizes.
 
Regulation shiftsThe algo rules are new and still changing. Defence: every order already carries its algo ID and leaves through a known IP; the broker partnership gives a registered path; legal counsel from stage 1.
 
Data costsLicensed real-time data can cost more than everything else. Defence: feed plugins, so we can switch vendors; tiered plans; users’ own broker data for their own live strategies.
 
Broker dependencyOne broker changing its API terms can cut off its users. Defence: several broker adapters by stage 2, and partners rather than scrapers.
 
Users losing moneyOption selling has fat tails, and losing users leave. Defence: honest backtests with walk-forward, risk defaults, and the shadow gap shown before anyone goes live.
 
Building too much too earlyThe final architecture is a 2-year target. Defence: the intermediate one first, and each swap waits for its trigger.

## What I wouldn’t do

- Chase HFT, co-location or kernel-bypass networking. Our users’ edge is discipline, not microseconds; the broker round trip dwarfs our latency.
 
- Become a broker. Partner with them; the licence and the capital tie the company down.
 
- Open a marketplace before the registration path for selling strategies is in place.
 
- Fight the no-code builders head-on. Offer templates on top of the SDK; our place is trust and depth.
 
- Rewrite strategies in Rust “for speed”. Strategies stay in Python; Rust goes where volume times per-message cost matters.
 
- Build the final architecture first. Contracts first, implementations when their triggers arrive.
 
- Let the UI or a strategy enforce risk. The order service does, every time.

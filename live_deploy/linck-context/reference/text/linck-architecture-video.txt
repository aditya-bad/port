Linck architecture: the narrated video, as text

The script of reference/explainers/linck-architecture (32 scenes). Each scene's
narration, in order. Numbers marked measured come from the prototype; targets
and estimates say so.

1. Linck, in three parts

This video walks through the Linck architecture. First the final design: every
service, what it is written in, and what it is responsible for. Then how
things flow through it. Then the smaller first version we build on the way
there, and what changes between the two.

Everything we write sits in one of three parts.

LinckTrade does the trading. It takes in market data, runs the strategies,
checks every order and sends it to a broker, and keeps the money records,
called the ledger.

LinckLens turns that data into what you look at: the screens, Geek mode
charts, reports, and live P&L.

Linck Platform is shared by both: the front door every request comes through,
accounts and logins, billing, and notifications.

One rule keeps the parts apart. Lens reads Trade’s data, but never writes it.
No screen ever writes an order, a fill or a position.

When you press Stop or Pause, Lens doesn’t change anything itself. It passes a
command to Trade, and Trade decides.

Three services get nicknames, because people will say them all day. Iceberg
runs the strategies. Heimdall, the gatekeeper, checks and places every order.
Kraken talks to the brokers, with one arm per broker. Every other service
keeps its plain name.

2. Words: how software runs

Some words first, so that nothing later is vague. These are about how our
software runs.

A service, or microservice, is a program that runs on its own, with one job
and its own data. Other services talk to it over the network. They never read
its tables.

A module is a part inside a program. It ships and runs with the program, but
keeps to its own tables, so it can be split out into a service later.

A container image is a program packed with everything it needs to run: a
Docker, or O C I, image. The same image runs on a laptop and in production.

Kubernetes runs containers on a group of machines, called a cluster, and
restarts them when they die. EKS is Amazon’s managed Kubernetes.

A pod is one running copy of a container. A Deployment keeps a number of
identical copies. A StatefulSet gives each copy a fixed name and its own disk.
A DaemonSet puts exactly one copy on every machine. And a Job runs once, then
finishes.

A node is one machine, a virtual server. A node pool is a group of identical
machines, kept for one kind of work.

A region is an AWS location, like Mumbai. A zone is a separate data centre
inside it. A cell is a full copy of the trading path for one group of
accounts, so a bad day stays inside one cell.

A sandbox is a fence around a strategy’s code. Inside, it can read its events
and send back commands, and nothing else: no network, no files, no threads.

3. Words: how the parts talk

Next, the words for how the parts talk to each other.

REST is the plain web way to ask for something: an H T T P request, with the
answer written as JSON text. Browsers speak it, so the screens use it.

S S E, server-sent events, is one long H T T P response that the server keeps
adding to. Screens use it for live updates.

gRPC is how our own services call each other: a typed request and answer, or a
stream of messages. Protobuf is its compact binary message format, with code
generated for every language.

mTLS, mutual T L S, means both ends of a connection prove who they are with
certificates, and everything on it is encrypted. Linkerd adds it between our
services.

An event log, or topic, is an append-only list of messages. Writers add to the
end; each reader keeps its own place, and can go back and read again.
Redpanda, which speaks Kafka’s protocol, keeps ours.

NATS is a fast messaging system. We use it for commands to the cells, and for
live updates where only the latest value matters. JetStream is its mode that
keeps messages until they are read.

Conflation means keeping only the newest value for a reader that has fallen
behind, instead of a queue of old ones.

A Temporal workflow is a long job written as steps. If a machine dies halfway
through, it resumes at the step where it stopped.

4. Words: trading

And the trading words.

A tick is one price update for one instrument: its last price, best bid and
ask, and volume. An instrument is anything with a price: the NIFTY index, a
future, or one option.

A strategy is code that decides trades. A deployment is one running copy of
it, with its own settings, in paper, shadow or live mode.

Paper means simulated fills only. Shadow means every order is also sent to the
simulator, so the two can be compared. Live means real orders, at a real
broker.

A batch is the bundle of new events a strategy gets at once: the latest
prices, its fills, its timers. It answers one batch before it gets the next.

An intent is what a strategy wants, like “buy one lot of this option”. An
order is an intent that passed the risk checks and went to a broker. A fill is
the broker saying it traded, in full or in part.

The ledger is the record of money: every order, fill and charge, written once
and never changed. Positions and cash are worked out from it.

A lease is a claim that must be renewed every few seconds: this machine runs
this deployment. The epoch counts how many times a deployment has started;
orders from an older epoch are refused.

A snapshot is a saved copy of a strategy’s state. The journal is every batch
since that snapshot. Together, they rebuild the strategy exactly.

A tenant is one customer of the platform: a person, or a partner broker with
its own users. Every row of data carries its tenant.

5. The final architecture, all of it

Here is the whole final architecture on one screen: twenty-one services, and
the stores underneath them. The lines between them come later, one flow at a
time.

Across the top is the outside world: you, on the web app, a phone, the
command-line tool or the SDK; the exchanges, whose prices reach us through a
licensed data vendor; and the brokers, who carry out the orders.

Linck Platform has four services: the edge gateway, identity, commerce, and
notifications.

LinckLens has four: the product API, the live gateway, read models, and the
assistant.

LinckTrade has thirteen. Five run once per region: control, the builder,
refdata, integrity, and the backtest farm.

Eight run inside every cell, on the trading path: market data ingest, the
sequencer, computed streams, the tick cache agent, Iceberg, Heimdall, Kraken,
and the sim venue. Each cell also has its own etcd, its own Redpanda, and its
own ledger.

The coloured badge on each service is its language. Rust, in red, is for the
hot path, where every microsecond and every byte counts. Go, in green, is for
services that must be correct and simple to run. Python, in yellow, is where
the product changes every day.

Underneath are the stores. Each holds one kind of data, and only the service
that owns it connects to it.

6. Where it runs

Everything runs on Amazon Web Services, in the Mumbai region, spread over
three zones. A warm standby in Hyderabad takes over if the whole region fails.

Each region has one platform cluster, on EKS. It runs everything that is not
on the trading path: the Platform and Lens services, Trade’s region services,
and shared systems like ClickHouse, NATS, Temporal and Vault.

Each cell is its own cluster, with three node pools. Market data machines are
pinned to their CPUs, and always on-demand: never spot machines, which Amazon
can take back.

Runtime machines have 64 virtual CPUs, a fast local NVMe disk for the journal,
and gVisor installed. They are tainted: marked so that Kubernetes places
nothing on them except the supervisor and the cache agent.

Strategies are not pods. Kubernetes only sees those two. The supervisor starts
every strategy itself, as a sandbox: an estimated three to five thousand on
one machine.

The services pool runs Heimdall, Kraken, the sim venue, and the cell’s own
etcd and Redpanda.

Amazon runs the heavy stores: Postgres on RDS, with a standby in another zone;
Redis on ElastiCache; S3 for files; and KMS for keys. Brokers only accept
orders from registered addresses, so each cell’s orders leave through NAT
gateways with two fixed IP addresses.

Releases come from Git. Each service becomes a distroless container image,
holding only the program and no shell, signed with cosign and stored in ECR,
Amazon’s image registry. Argo CD makes each cluster match the Helm charts in
Git, and Terraform builds the accounts, networks and clusters themselves.

And one rule for trading days: no production release between 09:00 and 15:35,
except an approved hotfix. Releases go out after 15:45, one cell at a time.

7. The stores, one job each

Now the stores. Every store has one job, and only the service that owns a
store connects to it. Nobody reads another service’s tables.

Postgres is the relational database, for anything that needs transactions.
Amazon RDS runs it, with a standby copy in a second zone. There is one
database per owner: control, a ledger shard for each cell, product, identity,
commerce, refdata, and integrity.

Each uses row-level security. Every transaction sets its tenant, and Postgres
hides every row that belongs to any other tenant, so a query that forgets its
filter still can’t see someone else’s data.

ClickHouse is a columnar database: it stores each column together, so it can
scan millions of rows a second for a chart. It holds bars, strategy decisions,
a copy of every fill, and the read models behind Geek mode and reports.

The lake is Parquet files on S3: every tick, option chain and fill, and the
reference data as it was on each day. They are organised as Apache Iceberg
tables, an open table format. Careful: that is a file format, not our Iceberg
service. Same word, two different things.

Redis, on ElastiCache, keeps small things that expire: login sessions, rate-
limit counters and small caches. Etcd keeps the leases and epochs in each
cell; every reader sees its changes in the same order, which is what a lease
needs. Vault and KMS keep broker tokens and signing keys, and only Kraken can
unseal a token.

Redpanda keeps the event logs where losing a message would cost money or
history: intents, orders, the ledger journal, the tick archive, and strategy
decisions. Each is ordered by its key: the account, the instrument, or the
deployment.

NATS carries commands to the cells, kept for a day, and live updates to
screens, where only the latest value matters. Temporal runs long jobs, like
the end-of-day chain, and resumes them where they stopped.

8. Linck Platform, inside

Linck Platform: four services that both other parts lean on.

The edge gateway is the only door in. It is Envoy, an off-the-shelf proxy,
plus an authentication plug-in we write in Go. It runs as a Deployment of
three or more copies, scaled by the number of requests.

A request arrives over HTTPS on port 443, and Envoy ends the encryption. The
plug-in checks who you are, from a session cookie, an API key or a login
token, and counts the request against your plan’s rate limit in Redis.

Then it routes: REST requests to the product API, live streams to the live
gateway, and gRPC from the SDK and the CLI to control. For the public API, it
also offers every gRPC method as REST.

Identity, in Go, owns users, tenants, API keys and roles, in its own Postgres
database, and the login sessions, which it keeps in Redis. It is a security
boundary, so it is kept small and audited on its own.

Commerce, in Go, is money coming in: plans, metering of what each tenant uses,
invoices, and later marketplace licences and revenue share. Its own database,
its own team.

Notifications, a Go consumer, reads events off the logs and sends them out:
push, email, Telegram, WhatsApp and webhooks, with templates per tenant. It
runs on its own, so a slow email provider can never back up anything else.

9. LinckLens, inside

LinckLens: four services that turn Trade’s data into what you see. None of
them can write an order, a fill or a position.

The product API, in Python, serves everything the screens read and write: the
Desk, reports, Geek mode data, journal notes, repair cases and preferences. It
is today’s API, kept, running three to twenty copies, scaled by CPU.

It reads charts and reports from ClickHouse, positions from a read-only
replica of the ledger, and deployment details from control over gRPC. The only
tables it writes are its own, in the product database.

A Stop or Pause button becomes a gRPC command to control. Trade does the rest.

The live gateway, in Go, holds up to a hundred thousand open screens. It
listens on NATS for live P&L, positions and alerts, and keeps one latest value
per screen, sent at most every 250 milliseconds over SSE. A slow phone gets
the newest number, never a backlog.

Read models, a Go consumer, reads the order, ledger and decision logs from
Redpanda, and builds the ClickHouse tables behind Geek mode, reports and
leaderboards. If a table is ever wrong, it is rebuilt by reading the logs
again.

The assistant, in Python, writes SDK code and explains a trading day. Its
tools are read-only, and its model calls have their own cost limits.

10. Control, builder, refdata

Now LinckTrade, starting with three of the services that run once per region.

Control, in Go, is the system of record for strategies and deployments. Its
registry holds every strategy and version. Each deployment moves from paper to
shadow to live, and can be started, stopped or paused.

It also holds each account’s risk limits, and its broker links: which account
trades at which broker. Three copies run over its own Postgres database.

Its scheduler is leader-elected: the three copies agree on one leader, and
only the leader places strategies. If the leader dies, another copy takes
over.

It packs deployments onto runtime machines by their expected CPU, from their
history, and prefers a machine that already follows the same instruments. Then
it sends the start command to the cell over NATS.

The builder, in Go, turns a user’s source code into a signed strategy
artifact. Each build runs as its own Kubernetes Job, inside gVisor, because
the code isn’t ours.

It builds in a pinned image per language, then certifies: the fault matrix of
crash and failure cases, a determinism double-run of the same day, and the
resource budget. A build that passes is signed with Sigstore and stored in the
artifact registry.

Refdata, in Go, is the instrument master: our id for every instrument, its lot
size, expiry, strike and freeze quantity, and its symbol at every broker. Each
morning it loads the exchange files and each broker’s list before 08:30.
Everyone reads it; only refdata writes it.

11. Market data in

Inside a cell, prices come in through market data ingest, written in Rust.
There is one ingest service per source: the licensed vendor feed, and a user’s
broker socket where their live trading needs it.

Each runs as a StatefulSet A and B pair: two copies on the same feed, so if
one dies, the other already has the data.

Inside, the socket client receives the source’s own format. The decoder turns
it into our record without allocating memory. The mapper swaps the source’s
symbol for our instrument id. And the tick filter drops bad prints: zero or
negative prices, out-of-order and duplicate updates, and spikes, held until
confirmed.

Our record is 96 bytes, the same in live trading, backtests and replays.
Prices are whole numbers of ten-thousandths of a rupee, so there is no float
rounding. Exchange time is the truth; receive time measures our own lag.

The sequencer runs as one active copy with a standby. It gives each
instrument’s updates a gap-free sequence number, so every reader can tell an
update it skipped from one that went missing.

When two sources carry the same instrument, it takes whichever arrives first
and records the other’s lag, so failing over costs nothing.

Then each update goes two ways: to the archive, the Redpanda topic
md.archive.v1 and then the lake, which keeps every tick; and out over gRPC
streams, to computed streams and to every runtime machine in the cell.

12. The latest price, never a backlog

Ticks follow one promise: a reader always gets the latest price, never a
backlog of old ones.

With a plain queue, a slow strategy falls behind and works through old prices.
In our prototype, measured: a strategy taking 40 milliseconds a decision, fed
a tick every 5 milliseconds, decided on prices three and a half seconds old,
with 474 ticks still waiting.

So every hop keeps one slot per instrument, overwritten by each update.
However slow the reader, the next thing it reads is the newest price. The same
strategy, measured: prices about 44 milliseconds old, with 500 updates skipped
and counted.

The rules that make it a promise. One batch in flight per reader. Skipped
updates are counted and reported, never hidden. A new reader first gets a
snapshot of everything it follows. An instrument that stops updating is marked
stale, never quietly repeated. And memory stays bounded: one slot per
instrument.

Some things are never skipped: fills, order updates, rejects, timers and
commands queue in order. Bars and indicators are built upstream from every
tick, so skipping can’t corrupt them, and the archive keeps every tick.

13. Computed streams

Computed streams, in Rust, works out what many strategies would otherwise each
compute on their own: bars, indicators, implied volatility and greeks, and
option chain views. It runs as an A and B pair in each cell.

Each series is a node in a DAG: a graph of calculations where each feeds the
next, and nothing loops back. A node’s id is a hash of its function, its
parameters and its inputs.

So when two strategies ask for EMA 20 on NIFTY’s one-hour bars, they get the
same node, computed once.

With each batch of updates, it recalculates only the nodes that changed, in
order. Bars close on exchange time, not when our machine happens to see the
tick.

Implied volatility uses Jäckel’s “Let’s Be Rational” method on the future’s
price, with the Black-76 model, and the greeks come from the same. Chain views
answer questions like “which strike is at the money, by the future” in memory.

Every output goes back out as a synthetic instrument, so a strategy subscribes
to EMA 20 exactly the way it subscribes to a price.

When a cell starts, it warms up from the lake, so an indicator is ready at
09:15, not twenty bars later.

14. The tick cache agent

On every runtime machine, the tick cache agent, in Rust, takes the streams and
writes them into shared memory: memory that the agent and the supervisor on
that machine both map. Reading a price is a memory read: no copy, no network.

There is one slot per instrument, 128 bytes, overwritten with each update. The
slot is the latest price by construction.

Slots use a seqlock. The writer bumps a version number to odd, writes, then
bumps it to even. A reader copies the slot, then checks the version didn’t
change; if it did, it simply reads again. Nobody ever waits on a lock.

Beside the slots is a ring of the last million updates, two to the twentieth,
with a cursor for each reader. A reader that falls a whole lap behind doesn’t
hold anyone up: it jumps to the slots, and is told how many it skipped.

It runs as a DaemonSet: exactly one copy on every runtime machine.

15. Iceberg: the strategy runtime

Iceberg is the strategy runtime: simple on top, deep underneath. On each
runtime machine, one supervisor, written in Rust, runs thousands of
strategies. It runs as a DaemonSet.

Each strategy runs in its own sandbox. Most are processes inside gVisor, a
kernel in user space from Google that stands between the code and the real
machine. So a strategy can be written with any SDK: Python, Go, Rust or
TypeScript.

Python strategies start by forking a zygote: a Python process with the SDK
already loaded. Each then costs about 2 megabytes instead of 52, measured.
WASM strategies run in Wasmtime, and remote strategies run on the user’s own
machine, over a gRPC stream.

When a strategy is ready, the batch builder makes its next batch from the
latest values in shared memory, plus every fill, order update and timer
waiting for it.

The protocol codec sends the batch as a protobuf message over a Unix socket.
The strategy answers with commands: orders, cancels, timers, and what it
decided and why.

The budget timer gives each batch 50 milliseconds before a warning, and one
second before the strategy is killed and restarted.

The journal writes each answered batch to a write-ahead log on the machine’s
local NVMe disk, shipped to S3 every second. The snapshotter saves the
strategy’s state every 100 batches, every 30 seconds, and before a move.

The lease keeper holds this machine’s lease on each deployment in etcd. The
intent writer puts every order intent on the Redpanda topic orders.intent.v1
before anything else happens, and decisions go to strategy.decision.v1.

Strategies must be deterministic: the only clock is ctx.now, the only
randomness is ctx.random, and Python’s hash seed is fixed. Replaying a day
gives the same decisions, and certification checks it by comparing hashes.

16. Heimdall: orders and risk

Heimdall, the gatekeeper, is orders and risk, in Go. It is the only thing that
writes orders, fills, positions and money. It runs as a StatefulSet of three,
each copy owning a share of the accounts.

Its intake reads intents from the log. Every intent has an id: the deployment,
the batch, and a number, the same on every replay. The id is unique in the
database, so a repeated intent finds its first order instead of making a
second.

The epoch fence refuses any intent whose epoch is older than the deployment’s
current lease: two machines can never trade one deployment.

Then the risk pipeline, in the same process, in under a millisecond. Static
checks: lot size, tick size, price band, freeze quantity, market hours.
Deployment checks: its lot and entry limits, and its own kill switch.

Account checks: margin, the daily loss limit, and the order rate. Global
checks: the tenant’s and the platform’s kill switches, and rules written in
CEL, a small expression language from Google. One rule overrides: an exit is
never blocked by cash or margin, only by the static checks.

The basket planner places trades with several legs. Hedge first, the default,
places the buy legs and waits for their fills before it sells; simultaneous
sends every leg at once, for exits. Every leg is a limit order with price
protection.

Each order moves through a state machine: new, risk checked, routed, open,
partly filled, filled; or rejected, cancelled, or expired.

The ledger writer records every change in the cell’s Postgres ledger shard as
double entry: each fill moves cash, position value and charges in lines that
sum to zero. Charges and slippage are lines of their own, and positions and
cash are worked out from the journal.

In the same transaction, it writes an outbox row. A relay publishes the outbox
to Redpanda, so nothing is ever published without being saved, or saved
without being published.

Every few minutes the reconciler compares positions with the broker’s; the
broker wins, and any difference becomes an issue. And in shadow mode, every
order is mirrored to the sim venue too, and both fills land in shadow fills,
to compare.

17. Kraken: the broker gateway

Kraken is the broker gateway, in Go: one code base, with an arm for each
broker. The same image is deployed once per broker in each cell, started with
a flag such as broker equals kite, so one broker’s outage, limits or IP rules
never touch another.

Logins and tokens: each broker’s login flow and its daily refresh. Tokens are
sealed in Vault, and opened only in this service’s memory.

The symbol map, loaded from refdata before 08:30, turns our instrument id into
the broker’s token and trading symbol, and the translator turns our order
types into the broker’s.

An order above the exchange’s freeze quantity is sliced into child orders
under the limit. And our order id goes in the broker’s tag field, so a retry
finds the first attempt instead of placing twice.

A token bucket per app key and per account keeps us under each broker’s
published rate limits.

The broker’s WebSocket or postbacks become one ordered stream of order updates
back to Heimdall. On reconnect, it asks the broker for every order tagged
today, and reconciles before anything new goes out.

Each adapter declares its broker’s capabilities, such as basket margin or
modify, and Heimdall plans around them. Most adapters run inside the service.
A broker whose SDK exists only in Python runs as a sidecar in the same pod,
about 60 microseconds away, measured.

Orders leave the cell through NAT gateways with fixed IP addresses, registered
with each broker.

18. The sim venue and the backtest farm

The sim venue, in Rust, is a pretend broker. It fills paper and shadow orders
from real market depth, with models for queue position and latency.

It speaks the same contract as Kraken, so Heimdall can’t tell paper from live.
It runs two to six copies, so paper load never slows a live order.

Its fill model lives in linck-core, a shared Rust library with a binding for
each SDK language, so live, backtests and local runs compute the same fills,
the same indicators, the same everything.

The backtest farm runs the same runtime and the same sim venue in batch, over
the lake: backtests, parameter sweeps, and replays. Each backtest is a
Kubernetes Job.

It runs on spot machines: spare AWS capacity at a fraction of the price, which
Amazon can take back. Karpenter, the node autoscaler, adds machines as the
queue grows: from nothing to 500 virtual CPUs in minutes.

19. Integrity

Integrity, in Go with Temporal workflows, checks that what happened is what
should have happened. It runs once per region, mostly after the market closes,
and owns its own Postgres database.

The day check replays each deployment on the broker’s one-minute candles after
the 15:45 export, and flags any day where the replay and what actually
happened differ.

Each difference becomes a repair case, with proof charts and the paths a fix
could take. Replays run as backtest-farm jobs, on the same Rust runtime as
live, so a replay is exact by construction.

It also compares shadow fills with real ones, reconciles positions with each
broker, and collects the issues strategies report about themselves.

Because the end-of-day chain is a Temporal workflow, a chain that stops
halfway resumes at the step where it stopped. The target: checks and repair
cases ready by 16:30.

20. Flow: a price reaches a strategy

Now the flows, one at a time. First, one price, from the exchange to a
strategy.

A trade happens on the exchange. The licensed vendor sends the new price over
its own TCP or WebSocket connection, and md-ingest decodes it into our 96-byte
record, maps it to our instrument id, and runs the tick filter.

Over gRPC, like every call between our services, the record reaches the
sequencer, which gives it the next sequence number for that instrument. A copy
goes to the archive topic on Redpanda, and from there to the lake.

Then it streams out over gRPC, to computed streams, which updates the bars,
the EMA and the greeks that depend on it, and to the cache agent on every
runtime machine. Each stream keeps only the latest value for a reader that has
fallen behind.

The cache agent writes the price into its slot in shared memory, over the last
one.

When the strategy is ready, the supervisor reads the latest values from shared
memory, builds the next batch, and sends it as protobuf over a Unix socket. A
strategy that was busy simply sees the newest price.

The target, from a tick to the strategy’s intent, is under 50 milliseconds at
the 99th percentile, inside a cell. That is a design target, not yet a
measurement.

21. Flow: an order, from decision to your screen

Next, one order: from a strategy’s decision to a fill on your screen.

The strategy answers its batch with an intent, say a hedged basket of two
legs. The supervisor writes it to orders.intent.v1 on Redpanda, keyed by
account, before anything else happens, so it can’t be lost.

Heimdall reads it. It checks the id for a duplicate and the epoch fence, runs
the risk pipeline, and records the order and an outbox row in the ledger
shard, in one Postgres transaction.

It calls Kraken over gRPC. Kraken translates the order, tags it with our order
id, and places it through the broker’s REST API, leaving through the cell’s
fixed IP. For a paper order, the sim venue fills it instead.

The broker’s update comes back over its WebSocket. Kraken turns it into one
ordered stream of order updates, and Heimdall records the fill in the journal,
with its outbox row, in one transaction.

The relay publishes the fill to orders.event.v1. Read models write it into
ClickHouse, for Geek mode and reports.

The cell’s log is copied to the region, the fill reaches the live gateway as a
live update on NATS, and it is pushed over SSE to your screen. Notifications
sends a push to your phone.

The target, from intent to broker, is under 30 milliseconds at the 99th
percentile. Again, a design target, not yet a measurement.

22. Flow: opening a screen

Opening a screen: the Desk, for example.

The browser asks the edge gateway over HTTPS: a REST request, with JSON. The
edge ends TLS, checks the session cookie against the session kept in Redis,
and counts the request against the plan’s limit.

It passes the request to the product API, which reads ClickHouse for the
charts, the ledger’s read-only replica for positions, and asks control over
gRPC for the deployments.

The answer goes back as JSON, and the screen draws.

The browser also keeps one SSE stream open, through the edge, to the live
gateway. From then on, live P&L, positions and alerts arrive on it: the latest
value, at most four times a second.

23. Flow: shipping a strategy

Shipping a strategy, from your laptop to running in a cell.

You run linck push. The command-line tool sends your source over gRPC, through
the edge, to the builder.

The builder starts a Kubernetes Job in gVisor, with the pinned build image for
your language. It builds, then certifies: the fault matrix, the determinism
double-run, and the resource budget. That takes minutes.

The passing artifact is signed with Sigstore and stored in the artifact
registry: your source, its lockfile and its manifest. It is not a container
image.

You press Deploy, or run linck deploy. Control checks that this version is
certified and within your limits, and its scheduler picks a machine with room
and the right instruments.

Control sends the start command over NATS, on the subject for that cell.

The supervisor on that machine fetches the signed artifact and checks its
signature, takes the lease in etcd with a new epoch, and starts the sandbox.

The strategy subscribes to its instruments, and the first batch arrives. From
here on, it is the price journey you saw before.

24. Flow: when things break

Now, what happens when things break. Four cases.

A strategy crashes. The supervisor starts a new process with the next epoch,
loads the last snapshot, and replays the journal since then, checking every
state hash. Orders it makes again carry the same ids, so Heimdall refuses them
as duplicates. The same crash on the same batch twice means quarantine: no new
entries and no restart loop, while square-off, stops and the kill switch still
work.

A machine dies. Its leases in etcd expire in about five seconds. The scheduler
grants them to another machine with the epoch plus one, which loads the
snapshot and journal shipped to S3 and catches prices up from its own cache,
in under two seconds.

A zombie: the old copy didn’t really die, and keeps sending intents. Each
carries the old epoch, so Heimdall’s fence refuses every one. In our
prototype, measured: a zombie on a feed three minutes late had all 134 of its
orders refused.

A broker goes down. Its Kraken deployment reports it; Heimdall stops new
entries for that broker’s accounts, queues their exits, and alerts. Other
brokers carry on. If a data vendor goes down, the other source already carries
the instruments.

25. Flow: the end of the day

And the end of each trading day, in order.

By 15:20, the 15:15 exits are drained. By 15:35, zero positions are left
unreconciled with the brokers.

At 15:45 the day’s data is exported, and from then on releases may go out, one
cell at a time.

Integrity’s end-of-day chain runs as a Temporal workflow: the day check
replays every deployment on the one-minute candles, and each difference
becomes a repair case.

The target: checks and repair cases ready by 16:30. Meanwhile the tick archive
keeps three days on Redpanda before it lives in the lake, and the read models
are already up to date.

26. From the final architecture to the first version

Here is the final architecture once more: twenty-one services, in three parts.
Now watch it fold down into the first version, the one we actually build
first.

The frames of the three parts go first. In the first version, the parts are
not yet separate services.

Five services wait for later, on the shelf at the top: commerce, read models,
the assistant, the builder, and the tick cache agent.

The edge gateway becomes a plain AWS load balancer, with TLS and cookie
sessions. It is not our code yet.

Notifications becomes a small Python consumer.

The product API, control, identity and refdata fold into one Python program,
core-api: a modular monolith, where each module keeps its own tables and never
joins another’s.

The three Rust market-data services fold into one Go service, md, with the
bars and chain views inside it.

Iceberg’s Rust supervisor becomes runner: Python workers, each holding a share
of the strategies.

Integrity and the backtest farm fold into Python Temporal workers.

Heimdall stays exactly as it is: the same Go service from day one. Kraken
stays too, as broker-gw-kite, with only Kite at first. The live gateway stays,
in Go. And the Rust sim venue becomes sim, a Python fill model.

All of it runs on one Kubernetes cluster, in one region, with one cell. And
the stores: Redpanda’s logs move onto NATS JetStream, etcd’s leases move into
Postgres, Vault waits for later, and the lake is plain Parquet files. That
leaves nine deployables of our own.

27. The first version: nine deployables

The first version has nine deployables of our own. Here is each one, with what
it is written in and what it does.

Core-api, in Python, is a modular monolith: one program, one team, one
database. Its modules are the product API for the screens, deployments, which
is control’s job, identity, and instruments, today’s resolver. Each module
owns its tables and never joins another’s, so splitting one out later is a
move, not a rewrite.

The live gateway, in Go, is the same service as in the final: SSE to screens,
latest values only.

Notifications is a small Python consumer: it reads events from NATS and sends
the alerts.

Md, in Go, takes Kite’s WebSocket and one licensed vendor, cleans the ticks,
builds bars and chain views inside itself, keeps the latest prices in Redis,
and streams ticks over conflated gRPC to the runner workers.

Runner, in Python, runs as a StatefulSet. Each worker holds a lease in
Postgres for its shard of strategies, keeps one latest slot per instrument,
and saves each strategy’s state as a deployment_state row. Strategies compute
their own indicators, and only Python strategies run, in gVisor.

The OMS, in Go, is Heimdall from day one: the single writer, the risk pipeline
and the outbox, writing the positions and lots tables in the one Postgres.

Broker-gw-kite, in Go, is Kraken with one arm: Kite. Paper orders are routed
to sim.

Sim, in Python, is a fill model behind the same broker contract that Kraken
speaks.

The workers are Python Temporal workers running today’s code, moved: the day
check, repair, replay and backtests.

All nine run on one EKS cluster in Mumbai, across three zones, with three node
pools: services, runtime with gVisor, and data. About six to ten machines,
sized for about two thousand strategies, five thousand instruments and twenty
thousand updates a second at the open: estimates. NATS JetStream on three
nodes carries every event; there is one Postgres, a primary and a replica, one
ClickHouse, Redis, and Parquet files on S3.

28. Swapped: the services

Now, what is swapped for what, and why. First the services. Each row: the
first version, the final, and the language change.

The edge: a plain load balancer first; Envoy with our Go plug-in when the
public API launches.

The control plane: Python modules inside core-api first; separate Go services
once marketplace and billing work begins, or a second backend team. They move
one module at a time: the gateway routes that module’s endpoints to the new Go
service.

The product API stays Python. It is the rest of core-api, and keeps serving
the web app.

Market data goes from Go to Rust, for the cost of every message times the
volume, and no garbage-collector pauses. The switch comes past ten thousand
instruments, a second vendor, or tick-by-tick feeds; both run on the same feed
for two weeks first.

Computed streams appear when the same EMA is being computed thousands of
times: when duplicate work passes thirty percent of runtime CPU.

The runtime goes from Python workers to Iceberg’s Rust supervisor when the SDK
opens to the public, for isolation per strategy, budgets, any language, and
faster recovery. Deployments move across by lease handoff at minute
boundaries.

Orders and risk is built once, in Go, and only grows.

The broker gateway is built once, in Go, and grows an arm per broker.

The sim venue goes from Python to Rust for the backtest farm, with fills equal
to the paisa on recorded days before the switch.

Integrity goes from Python workers to a Go service once the Rust runtime is
in, so replays run on the same runtime as live.

And notifications goes from Python to Go when partner brokers need their own
branding and channels.

29. Swapped: data, events and hosting

Then the data, the events and the hosting.

Events: NATS JetStream carries everything at first. Redpanda takes the orders,
fills, journal and ticks once retention outgrows JetStream, or more than five
services read the fills. The outbox relay writes both for a while.

The ledger: today’s positions and lots tables first; then a double-entry
journal written beside them, checked equal every night, until the tables
become views of it.

Snapshots: a Postgres row per deployment first; a local write-ahead log once
snapshot writes pass two hundred a second.

Leases: in Postgres first, in etcd with the Rust runtime.

The lake: plain Parquet files, later registered as Apache Iceberg tables, with
no rewrite.

Hosting: one cluster first; a cluster per cell with the first partner, or past
five thousand strategies, with a staff cell first.

And some things exist only in the final: Vault and KMS, the write-once audit
store, the assistant, commerce, the builder, the public API and the mobile
apps. They are added beside, and nothing is replaced.

30. Flows in the first version

The same flows, in the first version. The shape is the same; the pieces are
simpler.

A price: Kite’s WebSocket and the vendor send ticks to md, in Go, which cleans
them, builds bars and chain views, keeps the latest prices in Redis, and
streams ticks over conflated gRPC to each runner worker. The worker keeps one
latest slot per instrument, and each strategy computes its own indicators.

An order: the strategy returns an intent, and its runner worker publishes it
on NATS JetStream. The OMS checks it and records the order in the positions
and lots tables in one transaction, then calls broker-gw-kite over gRPC, which
places it at Kite. A paper order goes to sim instead.

The fill: Kite’s update comes back; the OMS records the fill and publishes it
on NATS, and the live gateway pushes it over SSE to your screen.

A screen asks core-api through the load balancer, over HTTPS, and core-api
reads Postgres and ClickHouse.

And a deploy: core-api’s deployments module checks the version and publishes a
start command on NATS. A runner worker takes the lease in Postgres and starts
the strategy, in gVisor.

31. The way there, one proven step at a time

Getting there. Every step ships alone, runs beside what it replaces until it
is proven equal, and leaves trading untouched.

Step P0 happens in today’s app: the tick broadcaster keeps one slot per
instrument for each subscriber, so a slow runner or screen gets the newest
tick. A slow-strategy test proves it.

I0 puts today’s app, unchanged, on one EKS cluster as core-api, proven by a
week of paper days beside the current machine. I1 adds the contracts
repository, and SDK version zero over today’s strategy base class. I2 moves
strategies out of the API process into leased Python runner workers, proven by
a week of identical fills.

I3 brings the Go OMS and the Kite gateway: first writing in shadow beside
today’s fill path, then the single writer once the journal and today’s tables
match every night for two weeks. I4 adds the Go market data service, proven by
bars equal to Kite’s one-minute candles. I5 adds Temporal for the end of day,
tenants with row-level security, and shadow mode.

Then the final pieces. F1: Rust feed handlers and the tick cache, beside the
Go service, until every record and bar is equal for two weeks. F2: computed
streams. F3: the Rust supervisor and sandboxes, with leases in etcd, until
decision hashes match the Python workers. F4: the Rust sim venue and the
backtest farm, with fills equal to the paisa.

F5: Redpanda for orders, fills, the journal and ticks. F6: the control plane
to Go, one module at a time, behind the Envoy gateway, and the public API. F7:
cells, each with its ledger shard and its own cluster. F8: the Go, Rust and
TypeScript SDKs, WASM and remote sandboxes, and the marketplace. F9: the
refdata service, and more brokers.

32. What never changes

Some things never change, from the first version to the final, because users
and strategies depend on them. So they are designed once, in the first
version.

The protobuf contracts, which only grow. Strategy code written against the
SDK. What an order, a fill and a position mean in the ledger. The web app. And
the decision log, strategy issues, and the repair model.

To recap: three parts, LinckTrade, LinckLens and Linck Platform. Three
nicknames: Iceberg runs the strategies, Heimdall guards every order, and
Kraken reaches the brokers. Twenty-one services in the final, nine in the
first version, and a proven step between them.
